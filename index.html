A goalkeeper rating that measures the goalkeeper, not the defense in front of her.

Field hockey still evaluates keepers with goals-against average, save percentage, and shutouts. Each of those is as much a team statistic as a keeper statistic. The Keeper Index is a composite rating built on four factors chosen so that a keeper on a losing team can be measured on the same footing as a keeper on a champion.

The problem

The NCAA publishes five goalkeeper leaderboards: goals-against average, save percentage, saves per game, shutouts, and win-loss percentage. Three of the five are dominated by the quality of the defense. A keeper who sees four shots a game behind a top-ten team will post a sub-1.00 GAA and a handful of shutouts regardless of how well she plays. A keeper who sees nine shots a game behind a struggling team will not, regardless of how well she plays.

All-conference voting follows those numbers. In 2026 the Ivy League's first-team goalkeeper ranked 22nd nationally in save percentage and 70th in saves per game. The honorable-mention keeper ranked 10th and 15th. Both outcomes follow from the statistics the voters have, which is the problem.

Ice hockey solved this between 2008 and 2020. Save percentage replaced GAA as the primary measure, then shot-quality adjustment replaced raw save percentage, then goals saved above expected replaced both. Soccer followed with post-shot expected goals. Every major goalkeeping sport now separates what the keeper did from what the defense allowed. Field hockey has not started down that road.

This site is a first attempt. It uses only published NCAA statistics and a full-season results file, so anyone can reproduce it. The lessons page explains what was borrowed and what could not be, since field hockey does not yet publish shot location.

The rating in one paragraph

Each qualifying keeper is ranked nationally on four factors: save percentage (efficiency), shots on goal faced per game (workload), goals saved above average per game (goals prevented, charged only on shots that reached her), and strength of schedule (NCAA RPI formula, computed from every D1 result). The four ranks are added. Lowest total ranks first. No weights and no tuning.

Why believe it

It was tested against the obvious alternative and the alternative failed.

The natural way to add goals against is to add goals against per game. That version was built and run. Its top ten was ten keepers from RPI top-20 teams, and its rank correlation with team RPI was 0.79. It reproduced the team standings. The four-factor version's correlation with RPI is 0.25, with 4 of the top ten from top-20 programs and 3 from teams with losing records. The full comparison is on the methodology page.

Everything is published.

Component values, component ranks, the SOS calculation, the 690-game results file, and the league-average save rate used for expected goals are all downloadable. If the method is wrong, the data to show it is here.

The author has a conflict of interest and says so.

The author's daughter is a Division I goalkeeper and appears in the rankings. The method was fixed before any rankings were produced and was not adjusted afterward. The rejected variants, including the one that ranks her lower, are published alongside the primary rating. Full disclosure.
